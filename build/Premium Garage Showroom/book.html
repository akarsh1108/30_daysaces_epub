Chapter

Measure the garage and the actual cars

This book helps a household plan a premium-looking garage that still parks real vehicles, stores bikes, and can be maintained after ordinary use. It is not a building-code manual, an electrical guide, a fire-code manual, a structural engineering text, or a coating specification. The primary reference is a private two-car garage. A 20-by-20-foot bay is an illustration, not a promise that two cars and four bikes will fit. Apartment, stilt, and shared parking are stricter: many buildings forbid drilling, coatings, or EV equipment. Some scenarios use Indian cities as compact-garage illustrations. They do not define the market. Prices are USD. Measurements are inches. Example dimensions are illustrations until you measure the actual garage and vehicles. Manufacturer instructions, the lease or HOA rules, and qualified professionals take precedence over this book. Use the book in this order. Assess: measure the actual garage and both vehicles. Plan: choose a layout that survives a parked-car test. Verify: structure, electrical capacity, moisture, permissions. Select: materials and vendors. Install: only after hold points are cleared, with qualified people for electrical and uncertain structure. Test: parking, doors, walking, bike retrieval, emergency access. Maintain. Chapters 1–7 are action. Chapters 8–12 are worksheets that copy those records — they are not a second project. Exhaust safety: never rely on a carbon monoxide alarm to make indoor vehicle operation safe. Do not run a vehicle in an enclosed garage. If exhaust fumes or an alarm indicate a possible hazard, leave the area and follow appropriate emergency guidance. An alarm is a warning device, not a substitute for preventing exposure. Ventilation requirements depend on the garage and local rules, especially when the garage connects to a home. Gasoline, solvents, paints, aerosols, and other flammables must follow product labels, local rules, and appropriate containers. Decorative bins are not chemical storage. Keep ignition sources away. Do not store unknown or unlabeled chemicals in a showroom garage. A fire extinguisher's type and placement depend on the actual hazards and local requirements; one extinguisher does not make every hazard safe. Keep emergency exits and access routes clear. For suspected fuel leaks, strong fumes, or fire hazards, leave, do not investigate with a flame or spark, and follow emergency guidance and the property's reporting path. Electrical installation, circuit sizing, protection, grounding, and EV charging equipment must be designed and installed by appropriately qualified professionals and inspected as local rules require. The reader may plan and document loads, locations, and questions. Do not install circuits, junction boxes, or charging equipment from this book. A load schedule is a planning list, not proof that a service or circuit is safe. Confirm service capacity and whether a parking space is authorized for charging before promising EV readiness. Do not route cables across vehicle paths. Conduit appearance cannot override electrical code. Equipment manufacturer requirements for mounting, ventilation, and clearances take precedence. Confirm the substrate, mounting method, manufacturer's load rating, and installation requirements for each mount. Do not infer structural suitability from wall appearance or from the weight of the bikes alone. A load-bearing wall is not automatic permission. Locate concealed utilities before drilling. Uncertain construction, overhead systems, or unusual loads need a qualified assessment. Four bike mounts are not automatically safe because the combined bike weight looks small. A layout that fits on paper may still be uncomfortable or unsafe. Do not mount anything until both actual cars have parked, opened, and released a walking path.

In this apartment

A US detached garage is labeled 20 by 20. After measuring to the lowest opener rail and both door tracks, the usable rectangle is smaller. One car is a compact hatchback; the other is a midsize SUV. Four bikes are planned for a side wall that sits in the SUV's door swing.

Record the actual garage and both vehicles. A labeled 20-by-20 bay is a reference, not proof that two cars and four bikes will fit.

Today's output: a filled Two-Car Fit Verification Sheet with both vehicles' length, width, mirror-to-mirror width, door-opening test, front and rear clearance, wall and bike clearance, walking route, and garage-door mechanism clearance.

Measure the interior at three points each way and keep the smallest numbers. Record clear height to the lowest obstruction: opener motor, tracks, torsion hardware, beams, water heater, electrical panel, steps, columns, ramps. Capture door opening width and where the open garage door sits. The paper size of the building is not the usable envelope.

Then measure each car: overall length and width, mirror-to-mirror width, and the space needed to enter and exit on the sides you actually use. Compact hatchbacks, sedans, SUVs, and larger vehicles are not interchangeable. Illustrative catalog ranges in this chapter are starting points only. Park both actual cars, open the doors, open the trunk or hatch, walk to the house or exit, and note every squeeze. If the sequence requires unsafe pinching or repeated repositioning, revise the layout before buying mounts.

Wall-mounted bikes steal door swing and walking width. Handlebars, pedals, and racks count. Emergency access paths must stay clear of vehicles, storage, and display. Parking aids must not become trip hazards. Apartment or stilt parking may forbid this entire project; confirm permissions before measuring for a showroom.

Do this

  1. Measure usable interior width, depth, and lowest height.
  2. Record Car A and Car B length, width, mirrors, and a real door-opening test.
  3. Park both cars and walk the exit path before choosing a wall for bikes.

Where this goes wrong

Using a 20-by-20 label, a 36-inch walkway rule, or a 24-inch front clearance as if they were universal, then mounting bikes into a door arc.

Today's output

  • Fit sheet filled
  • Both cars parked and tested
  • Unresolved constraints listed

Chapter

Bike walls, mounts, and structure

Confirm the substrate, mounting method, manufacturer's load rating, and installation requirements for each mount. Do not infer structural suitability from wall appearance or from the weight of the bikes alone. A load-bearing wall is not automatic permission. Locate concealed utilities before drilling. Uncertain construction, overhead systems, or unusual loads need a qualified assessment. Four bike mounts are not automatically safe because the combined bike weight looks small. This chapter chooses mount types and a verification path. It does not certify that your wall can hold four bikes.

In this apartment

A villa garage has wood studs. A stilt parking stall in an apartment building has a concrete wall, a waterproofing layer, and a lease that forbids drilling. Both owners want four bikes on display. Only one of those walls may be a legal or structural candidate.

The wall you have — not the bike weight — decides whether a mount is allowed.

Today's output: a substrate map (stud, masonry, unknown), a permission note, and a mount type per bike that the manufacturer lists for that substrate — or a decision to store bikes on the floor or elsewhere.

Private villa garages and shared apartment parking are different projects. Shared stalls often prohibit wall systems, overhead storage, and any penetration of waterproofing. If permission is missing, stop this chapter.

Choose vertical hooks, cradles, or folding arms only from products rated for the bike and the substrate. Follow the manufacturer's hardware, embedment, and spacing. Do not invent a 3-to-4 safety factor or a 66-to-74-inch hook height as a universal rule. Heights must clear the garage-door travel, the opener, and the people who lift the bikes. Handlebar, pedal, and rack projection must stay out of the verified car-door arcs from the fit sheet.

Locate electrical, plumbing, and reinforcement before drilling. If the wall construction is unknown, the load is unusual, or you want overhead storage, get a qualified assessment. Do not perform improvised overload tests that can damage the building. Use only the manufacturer's installation and inspection method.

Do this

  1. Identify substrate and permission.
  2. Match each mount to the manufacturer's substrate list and load rating.
  3. Mock the bike outline against the parked-car door swing before drilling.

Where this goes wrong

Mounting four bikes because the combined weight 'looks fine,' or using drywall as structure.

Today's output

  • Substrate named
  • Permission recorded
  • Each mount has a product rating

Chapter

Layout options and the daily-use test

Plans A, B, and C are alternatives for different constraints, not a universal best layout. The showroom must survive rain, groceries, bike retrieval, and a blocked car.

In this apartment

Both cars are in. It is raining. Groceries need to come out of the hatch. A bike is needed from the wall. The house door is behind Car B.

Test the actual sequence: enter, park both, open doors, walk to the exit, retrieve a bike, return it. If any step is unsafe, change the layout before installation.

Today's output: a chosen layout option — A side-wall bikes, B back-wall bikes, or C split walls — plus a written daily-use pass/fail for rain parking, groceries, bike retrieval, tool access, and cleaning.

Option A puts bikes on a side wall when that wall is not in a primary door swing. Option B uses the back wall when the garage door tracks eat the sides. Option C splits bikes when no single wall is clear. None is 'the best layout.' Choose from the fit sheet. If two cars cannot open and a person cannot walk to the house without crossing an active vehicle path, reduce display, store bikes elsewhere, or park one car outside.

Minimum clear floor is whatever the movement test needs, not an aesthetic margin. Keep a tool and pump cabinet on a path that does not require moving a car. Seasonal gear leaves the floor. Display only items that still look acceptable after a wet week; everything else gets closed storage or leaves the garage.

Garage-door travel, sensors, emergency release, and vehicle height clearance are part of the layout. Storage must not sit in the door's path. Child and visitor safety is not guaranteed by a pretty layout: chemicals stay in labeled, appropriate storage; tools do not live on the floor.

Do this

  1. Run the full movement sequence with both actual cars.
  2. Choose A, B, or C only if the sequence passes.
  3. Name a tool location that does not require moving a car.

Where this goes wrong

Installing the gallery wall first, then discovering groceries cannot leave the hatch.

Today's output

  • Movement sequence passed
  • Layout letter chosen
  • Tool access without moving a car

Chapter

Floors, walls, and finish care

Coatings and panels are selected for the actual slab, moisture, and chemicals you will use. They are not waterproofing, and they are not maintenance-free.

In this apartment

Rain sheets in under the garage door and pools at the back wall. The owner wants a metallic epoxy because it photographs well.

Choose a coating from the manufacturer's substrate, moisture, and chemical limits. Coatings do not repair slope, leaks, or structural water.

Today's output: a moisture and slope note, a coating system name that matches those conditions, or a hold until water is resolved.

Check existing slope and where water goes. Rain at the entrance, wash water, and condensation are different loads. If water pools, find the source. Report persistent ingress to the owner or a qualified professional. Do not coat over an unresolved water problem.

Follow the coating manufacturer's tests, prep, temperature, and re-entry times. Ask the vendor which chemicals the finish can tolerate — brake fluid, gasoline, and cleaners differ. A friction number quoted in a brochure is not a universal wet-safety rating for your shoes and slope.

Protect finished floors during washing and jack use as the manufacturer allows. Vehicle washing may be restricted by local rules and by the coating warranty.

Do this

  1. Record slope, pooling, and moisture-test method from the product.
  2. Stop if water ingress is unresolved.
  3. Select a system only after the manufacturer conditions are met.

Where this goes wrong

Using a floor coating as waterproofing, or skipping moisture testing because the slab looks dry.

Today's output

  • Slope and pooling noted
  • Product moisture limits checked
  • Hold or proceed written

In this apartment

A renter wants slatwall on what looks like a solid wall. The substrate is unknown.

Panel appearance is not a load rating. Backup, fasteners, and the product listing decide.

Today's output: wall finish choice plus a backup method listed by the panel manufacturer, or a no-drill alternative.

Paint and panels in a garage see moisture, impact, and UV at the door. Use products rated for that exposure. Do not fasten bike loads to drywall alone.

If you do not own the building, prefer removable systems and get permission in writing. Replacement: keep extra slats or paint. Inspect fasteners on a schedule from the manufacturer, not 'when it looks loose.'

Cross-reference the substrate map from the wall chapter. This day selects finishes; it does not re-engineer the wall.

Do this

  1. Choose a garage-rated finish.
  2. Confirm backup and fasteners from the product.
  3. Record a no-drill fallback.

Where this goes wrong

Screwing slatwall to drywall because the bikes are 'only road bikes.'

Today's output

  • Finish chosen
  • Backup specified
  • Permission or fallback

In this apartment

A coastal garage rusts cheap brackets. A wood bench wicks wash water.

Name the exposure each finish must survive. 'Durable' without conditions is not a specification.

Today's output: one metal family and one wood or wood-look product, each with a cleaning method and a replacement plan.

Metal: corrosion, cleaner chemistry, and finish compatibility. Powder coat and stainless still fail if the cut edges rust. Clean with products the finish maker allows.

Wood: moisture, finish care, lift off the floor, and impact. Engineered products still swell if edges are open. Keep wood out of the wash zone.

Trim and transitions must not become trip hazards. Replace damaged pieces; do not caulk over a rocking reducer.

Do this

  1. Pick one metal finish for visible hardware.
  2. Pick wood that can live above splash.
  3. Write cleaning and replacement notes.

Where this goes wrong

Calling the package maintenance-free, then parking a wood bench in the wash stream.

Today's output

  • Metal family
  • Wood care note
  • Trip-hazard check on transitions

Chapter

Lighting layers without glare

Ambient, task, and accent lighting do different jobs. Footcandle targets in this book are illustrations. Fixtures must suit a garage environment. Controls must fail to a usable light.

In this apartment

A polished hood mirrors a downlight into the driver's eyes. The workbench is dim. The smart scene fails and the garage goes dark.

Verify uniformity and glare on actual paint and glass. Scenes must fail safe.

Today's output: a three-layer plan — ambient for moving around, task for work and loading, accent for bikes — plus a glare test on both parking spaces and a fail-safe (manual switch or always-on path light).

Ambient covers walking and parking. Task covers the bench, hatch, and lock area. Accent is optional. Compare the same vehicle under ambient only; ambient plus task; then all three. If accent lighting hides a puddle or a bike pedal, it is in the way.

Illustrative planning bands such as 20–30 footcandles ambient or 50–75 at a bench are starting points, not code. Choose levels for the activity, fixture layout, and any applicable requirements. Keep color temperature consistent unless a product requires otherwise. Use garage-rated, damp-or-wet-listed fixtures where the environment needs them.

Do not tie life-safety response solely to a lighting scene. If automation dies, a regular switch must still light the path. Electrical work remains professional installation.

Do this

  1. Place ambient so both parking spaces are usable.
  2. Add task light at the bench and loading side.
  3. Aim accents, then look for paint and glass glare. Confirm a manual-on path light.

Where this goes wrong

Programming a showcase scene as the only way to turn the lights on, or treating a footcandle table as a required installation.

Today's output

  • Three layers named
  • Glare test done
  • Fail-safe switch exists

In this apartment

Chapter

Budget, vendors, and what to postpone

A premium finish is optional. Safety, structure, electrical, and preparation are not. If the budget cannot cover the essential work, postpone the aesthetic upgrade.

In this apartment

A Good/Better/Best sheet prices epoxy and slatwall. It omits an electrician, moisture mitigation, permits, and disposal.

The cheapest quote is not comparable until scope, qualifications, and warranty are the same.

Today's output: a budget that includes design, professional assessments, demolition, prep, materials, labor, electrical, permits, delivery, disposal, contingency, and maintenance — with a go/postpone decision.

USD line items belong in writing. Warranty terms are only real when written. Lead times change. Installation quality is part of the bid. Check licenses separately from a vendor score.

If money is short, keep parking function, fire and exhaust rules, and required electrical corrections. Drop metallic floors, accent lighting, and extra bikes.

Renter and HOA fees, deposits, and restoration at move-out belong in the budget if you do not own the building.

Do this

  1. Fill every cost category, including labor and contingency.
  2. Score vendors on written warranty and qualifications, not photos.
  3. Postpone aesthetics if essential work does not fit.

Where this goes wrong

Spending the contingency on a Best-tier floor while the panel still needs professional work.

Today's output

  • Complete cost categories
  • Written warranties
  • Postpone-or-proceed decision

Wall-system bill of materials

The BOM is a buying list tied to substrate and ratings. It is not a second wall-design chapter.

In this apartment
