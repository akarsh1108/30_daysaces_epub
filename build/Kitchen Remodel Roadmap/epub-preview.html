Chapter

One cap, one contingency rule

This book is a kitchen planning roadmap for compact US homes and rentals. It is not a building-code manual, an electrical or gas guide, a plumbing license, a structural design, or legal advice. Some scenarios use apartments in Indian cities as compact-space illustrations. They do not define the market. Prices are in US dollars. Measurements are in inches. Example dollar amounts are illustrations, not typical remodel prices. 1 inch equals 25 mm if you need a conversion. Manufacturer instructions, the lease, building rules, licensed trades, and the authority having jurisdiction take precedence. You may plan, measure, document, and photograph. Qualified professionals perform electrical, gas, structural, and permit-required plumbing work. A score, a heat map, or a contingency formula in this book does not authorize unsafe work. Contingency lives inside the budget cap. It is not added on top. There is one method in this book. Ignore older notes that used a separate 8 percent plus risk-score formula, a competing 10–12 percent band, or a 12–15 percent band as a second calculator.

Day 2 · One contingency method, inside the cap

Day 2

One contingency method, inside the cap

In this apartment

Kitchen measurement error: two formulas on the same sheet produced two different leftover amounts.

Reserve twelve percent of the cap as contingency. Cut other lines if risk is high. Do not add contingency on top of the cap.

Today's output: the same cap, with working cost at eighty-eight percent and contingency at twelve percent, summing to one hundred percent of the cap. Category lines under working cost must add up to the working-cost total. Contingency is not copied into those categories.

Worked illustration, not a quote: cap 10,000 USD. Working cost 8,800 USD. Contingency reserved 1,200 USD. Example cosmetic split of the 8,800: demolition and waste 900, qualified fixture electrical or plumbing 2,000, finishes 2,200, fixtures 2,400, permits and fees 600, tools 700. Those six lines equal 8,800. Plus 1,200 reserved equals the cap. Do not also add 10–12 percent on top.

People overrun 15–25 percent when they have no cap and they spend the reserve during planning. Contingency is not a forecast of that overrun. If unknowns are high (old plumbing, moisture, complex permits), rescope first. If you still proceed, raise the reserved percent only by reducing other categories so the cap does not move. Do not use 8 percent plus half a percent times a risk score.

Do this

  1. Enter cap, working cost, reserve
  2. Make categories sum to working cost
  3. Cross out extra formulas

Where this goes wrong

Running the risk-score formula and the 12 percent rule on the same cap.

Today's output

  • Enter cap, working cost, reserve
  • Make categories sum to working cost
  • Cross out extra formulas

Renters, buildings, and local rules

Permit requirements vary by jurisdiction and project scope. Building-owner or association approval may be required even when a municipal permit is not. Electrical, gas, structural, and plumbing work must be assessed against applicable local requirements. This book is not a substitute for official approval or professional code interpretation.
