Chapter

The Under-$200 Weekend Alcove Makeover Blueprint

By the end of this chapter, you will have a written scope: the space you control, the appliance setup you will build around, and a millwork budget in US dollars. This is a US-focused book. All prices are USD. Example dimensions are inches. The millwork envelope is $200 unless you raise it on purpose.

In this apartment

Riya rents a one-bedroom in Austin. Her laundry sits in a 60 in wide alcove. She can add shelves and a folding counter if they stay removable, but she cannot move plumbing, add a dryer vent through an exterior wall, or drill a load-bearing concrete wall without written permission.

Write the space, the machines, and the money before you buy a board.

This book is millwork around machines that already exist: a folding counter, shelves, and labeled storage. It is not a license to install a washer, dryer, gas line, new drain, or new dedicated circuit. Hire a licensed plumber or electrician for those. A renter usually may use freestanding or limited-fastener millwork if the lease and HOA or landlord rules allow it. An owner may fasten into suitable structure after checking services in the wall.

All prices in this book are US dollars (USD). The worked millwork envelope is $200 for sheet goods, lumber, fasteners, primer, paint, caulk, and consumables for one compact alcove. It does not include the washer or dryer, plumber or electrician fees, appliance delivery, stacking kits, or tools you already own. If you use the worksheet outside the United States, convert the $200 envelope and check local product names and sheet sizes.

Pick one construction level before you shop. No-drill: freestanding frames, tension poles only where the maker rates them, and nothing that depends on wall screws. Limited-fastener: a few screws into identified structure, with landlord permission, a removal plan, and a load you can actually support. Permanent: owner approval, and professional help if the wall, vent, or electrics are uncertain. Removable is not the same as renter-safe. A frame that can tip, overload hollow plaster, or block hose shutoffs is not a weekend win.

Do this

  1. Write one sentence: balcony utility, internal wet area, or dedicated laundry closet — and whether you rent or own.
  2. Write the millwork envelope in USD and list what it excludes (machines, trades, tools).
  3. Circle no-drill, limited-fastener, or permanent before you sketch shelves.

Where this goes wrong

Starting with a Pinterest shelf height, then discovering the lid on a top-load washer cannot open.

Output

  • Space and tenure written down
  • $200 millwork envelope defined, exclusions listed
  • Construction level chosen

Shelf heights and counter spans follow the machines, not the other way around.

Select the appliance arrangement before you choose shelf heights, countertop dimensions, or support locations. Front-load washer: door swing, loading access, service clearance, and vibration. Top-load washer: lid opening height, overhead clearance, and access to controls. Stacked washer and dryer: only an approved stacking kit for those exact models, vertical load path, and service access to both units. Side-by-side machines: width, side clearance, shared folding surface, and both door swings. Washer only: leave a future dryer path if you might add one, plus hose and drain access.

A stacked pair is not a side-by-side pair with the dryer lifted. Stacking changes the load on the floor, the reach to the dryer door, and the service path. Do not invent a homemade shelf to carry a dryer unless that arrangement is specifically designed and approved for that purpose. Do not put a washer or dryer on an improvised countertop.

A French cleat is two interlocking strips, usually wood, cut at about 45 degrees: one on the wall, one on the furniture. A cleat (without “French”) is simply a strip the shelf sits on. You will use those words later. First, write which of the five setups you have.

Do this

  1. Circle one: front-load, top-load, stacked, side-by-side, or washer only.
  2. If stacked, confirm a maker-approved stacking kit exists for both model numbers.
  3. If top-load, measure lid arc before any overhead shelf height.

Where this goes wrong

Drawing a 36 inch counter over a top-load washer whose lid needs more than that to open.

Output

  • Appliance setup selected
  • Stacking kit confirmed or stacking rejected
  • No appliance sitting on improvised millwork

Generic alcove numbers are planning starts. The installation manual is the rule.

Record washer and dryer make and model. Obtain the installation manuals. Record required rear, side, top, and front clearances. Confirm ventilation and exhaust requirements where a dryer is present. Confirm water inlet, drain, and electrical connection locations. Confirm the service and removal path. Check whether a stacking kit is approved for those exact models.

A shelf, countertop, or enclosure must not reduce required ventilation, access, or manufacturer-specified clearances. If the manual and this book disagree, follow the manual. If you cannot find the manual, stop millwork that sits within 4 inch of the machine until you have it.

Electrical modifications, new outlets, and uncertain wiring belong to a qualified electrician. Never run a washer or dryer on an unsuitable extension cord. Keep water connections and electrical components arranged to the applicable electrical safety rules — this book does not publish a generic outlet-clearance number for every jurisdiction.

Do this

  1. Photograph the model stickers and save the PDFs of both installation manuals.
  2. Copy the maker's clearance table into your measurement sheet — do not invent substitutes.
  3. Mark shutoff valves, drain, vent termination, and the plug so millwork cannot bury them.

Where this goes wrong

Building a pretty box that covers the shutoff, the lint path, or the plug.

Output

  • Make, model, and manuals on the clipboard
  • Clearances copied from the maker, not from memory
  • Service path still open on the sketch

Chapter

Space, Machines, And Materials

By the end of this chapter, you will have a verified appliance layout and a material direction that matches moisture, not a single “best” board. Cross-reference Chapter 1’s setup choice instead of re-deciding it.

In this apartment

Ravi’s Austin alcove is 48 in wide and 24 in deep to the plaster, but a drain box steals 3/4 in on the left. The walls are not square.

The smallest width, the shallowest depth, and the maker’s rear clearance govern the build.

Measure width at the floor, at mid-height, and near the top. Measure depth at left, center, and right. Measure height at the back and the front. Use the smallest width and the shallowest depth for cuts. Baseboards, tile lips, and hose boxes shrink the usable opening.

Hoses bulge. Walls bow. Doors swing. If you guess, you will cut shelves that jam at the baseboard and a counter that blocks the drain. Keep plumbing and electrical where they are unless a licensed trade is moving them.

Immediate function for most US apartment laundry closets is a folding surface you do not sit appliances on, two or three shelves, and labeled bins. That is the millwork envelope. It is not a full laundry-room renovation.

Where this goes wrong

Measuring only at chest height, then discovering the baseboard is 5/8 in thicker than the wall above.

Output

  • Width, depth, and height recorded at multiple points
  • Smallest width and shallowest depth circled for cuts
  • No plumbing or electrical moves on the DIY list

No sheet good is universally right for a damp laundry alcove.

Plywood (typically 3/4 in for a folding counter, 1/2 in or 3/4 in for shelves): better moisture behavior than raw MDF if edges are sealed; still needs edge protection and a washable finish. Medium-density fibreboard (MDF): smooth for paint, cheaper, swells fast if edges stay raw — poor as an unsealed counter in a wet alcove. Melamine-faced board: wipeable face, vulnerable at every cut edge unless banded. Finish: bonding primer on every cut, then satin or semi-gloss enamel; add a water-based polyurethane on a folding counter if the product allows it over paint.

Shelves hold bottles and baskets. A folding counter is a work surface for laundry, not a stand for a washer or dryer unless the whole assembly is designed and approved for that load. Concentrated loads — a person leaning, a wet basket — matter more than a uniformly distributed textbook load.

Choose by moisture exposure, ventilation, the load, and how well you will seal edges. Skip exotic boards for a weekend envelope. Skip untreated particle board in the splash zone.

Where this goes wrong

Using raw MDF as a splash-zone counter because it was cheaper per sheet.

Output

  • Counter material and thickness written
  • Shelf material written, with edge-seal method
  • No appliance load assigned to an improvised top

In this apartment

Aditi’s Brooklyn wet area has a window on one side. She already chose a front-load washer with a ventless dryer stacked on a factory kit. Overhead shelves sit beside the stack, not over the dryer exhaust path — there is no exhaust duct, but the kit still needs the maker’s top clearance.

Zones follow doors, lids, vents, and shutoffs.

Put detergent within a short reach of the washer door or lid. Keep tall bottles where they cannot fall onto controls. Draw the door or lid arc on the sketch. Leave the shutoff valves, drain hose, and plug reachable without unscrewing millwork.

Vented dryers need a clear exhaust path; ventless dryers do not use that duct, but they still have maker clearances and lint access. Do not assume every US home has the same dryer type. A shelf near an exhaust route is allowed only if the installation instructions still work.

Bring the sketch to the home center. Do not cut to an example width from later chapters until this sketch matches your smallest measurements and the manuals.

Where this goes wrong

A beautiful overhead box that the stacked dryer cannot be removed through.

Output

  • Door or lid arc drawn
  • Shutoffs, drain, plug, and lint access marked reachable
  • Dryer type (vented, ventless, or none) written

Chapter

Building A Sturdy Laundry Countertop

By the end of this chapter, you will know what the counter is for, which material and thickness you will use, and that any span example is illustrative — not a universal design rule. Output: a counter decision, not a cut yet.

Name the job of the top before you name the span.

Most readers in this book need a folding and sorting surface over or beside machines, supported on wall cleats or a freestanding frame. That is not the same as a structural shelf carrying a dryer. Do not support appliances on an improvised shelf or countertop unless the arrangement is specifically designed and approved for that purpose.

Identify the material, thickness, support spacing, and attachment method. A common US-available starting stack for a folding top is 3/4 in plywood, sealed edges, with cleats on the back and both sides where the walls and construction level allow. That stack is a starting description, not an engineered rating for every width.

The cut-list matrix later in the book is illustrative. Do not cut materials using example dimensions without measuring your own alcove and confirming the appliance-specific requirements.

Where this goes wrong

Treating a 65 inch Pinterest span as a safe rule for 1/2 in MDF on two drywall anchors.

Output

  • Counter job named: folding surface, not appliance stand
  • Material, thickness, and support method written
  • Example cut lists flagged as not final

In this apartment

Sneha shops a Seattle home center, then a hardware aisle, then a paint aisle — using the lumber-hardware-paint loop.

One loop: sheet goods, lumber, fasteners, then finish.

Walk once. Sheet goods and any shop cutting first. Then battens for cleats. Then screws, wall anchors matched to the substrate you identified, and sandpaper. Then primer, enamel, and mildew-resistant caulk. Home-center aisle maps and SKUs vary by store and region. Ask what the merchant will rip, what minimum strip width they will cut, and how you will get a 4 × 8 ft sheet into the elevator or up the stairs.

Carry the bill of materials and the smallest alcove width. Reject warped boards by sighting down the edge. If the shop cannot cut below a stated width, plan those rips at home with a guide, or change the design.

The later shopping chapters repeat this workflow with local store categories. Skip any US store name you still see in an old figure caption — the rule is the workflow, not the brand.

Where this goes wrong

Buying decorative brackets first and discovering you still have no sheet for the top.

Output

  • Shop order written: sheets, lumber, fasteners, finish
  • Cutting limits asked before you pay
  • Transport into the building checked

Seal every cut. Thin coats. Respect cure time — it can spill past Sunday.

Prime every raw edge and cut face. End grain on plywood drinks primer; give it a second coat. Then a satin or semi-gloss enamel that the label allows in a humid room. Caulk wall joints with a flexible, mildew-resistant product after primer and before or after the last paint coat as the caulk label directs.

Water-based products often recoat the same day; they still need the labeled dry time before you load wet laundry on the top. If the weekend ends before cure, that is a successful minimum-viable weekend: parts cut and sealed, installation postponed. Do not promise every finish stack in 48 hours.

Caulk and a drip pan protect surfaces. They are not a substitute for sound inlet hoses, reachable shutoffs, and a correctly placed drain hose.

Where this goes wrong

One thick enamel coat Saturday night, then stacking detergent bottles on a tacky film Sunday morning.

Output

  • Edge-seal method chosen
  • Labelled dry and cure times copied onto the schedule
  • MVP option written if cure overruns the weekend

In this apartment

Karan’s Austin alcove is 65 inch between plasters. That number is his measurement, not a national standard.

Do not treat one span example as a universal design rule.

This book does not certify a maximum safe span for every plywood grade, every fastener, and every wall. State a span only as an example tied to a described stack. Example, not a rule: an 3/4 in plywood folding top, cleated on three sides into identified structure, with a front edge band, is a common way readers stiffen a roughly 1500–65 inch opening. If your opening is wider, your substrate is hollow plaster, or you only have two fasteners, this example does not apply.

Account for concentrated loads: a person leaning to fold, a full wet basket, someone sitting on the edge. If the top might be used as a seat or a step, stop — that is a different structure.

Add a center support or a shallow divider only when you have a wall, floor, or frame that can take that load without blocking hoses, vents, or machine removal. Recalculate after you measure. When in doubt, shorten the span or hire a carpenter who will stamp the method they use.

Where this goes wrong

Copying “over 62 inches add a center cleat” onto a 1/2 in MDF top on plastic anchors.

Output

  • Example span labeled illustrative
  • Concentrated loads considered
  • Centre support only if it does not block service

Chapter

From Measurements To A Working Sketch

By the end of this chapter, you will have a completed measurement sheet and a fastening plan matched to the wall you actually have. Output: numbers that feed the cut list, not the cut list itself.

In this apartment

Priya’s Austin rental alcove, side-by-side compact machines, limited-fastener permission for two studs only if they exist — she will verify the substrate before drilling.

The governing width is the smallest of three; the governing depth is the shallowest of three.

Worked example (illustrative, then you replace every number). Width: 59 inch at the floor, 60 inch at mid-height, 59 inch at the top because of a trim lip. Governing width: 59 inch. Depth: 32 inch at center, 30 inch at the right because of trim. Governing depth: 30 inch. Height: 216 inch clear under the soffit. Washer: 27 inch wide, 82 in high, 27 inch deep cabinet, plus the hose bulge the manual names. Dryer beside it: same cabinet size. Door swing: 90 degrees, needs 2 in extra at the left casing.

Plumbing: shutoffs 16 inch left of center, 14 inch AFF (above finished floor). Drain standpipe behind the washer. Outlet on the right wall, keep a panel off it. No dryer duct — ventless dryer. Service path: both machines pull straight out 32 in. Those figures belong to this example. Yours come from your tape and your manuals.

How this turns into millwork: a folding top cannot be 59 inch if you need a scribe gap and you must not pinch the right-hand door. A rear cleat cannot cover the standpipe. Shelf depth starts from governing depth minus the maker’s rear clearance, not from a catalogue “24 inch” top. The cut-list chapter will show example parts for a similar width and will tell you to recalc.

Where this goes wrong

Using the mid-height 60 inch as the cut width, then jamming the top on the trim lip.

Output

  • Three widths, three depths, two heights written
  • Governing (smallest/shallowest) values circled
  • Shutoffs, drain, outlet, and pull-out path on the sketch

A load-bearing wall is not automatically the right place for the fastener you have in the drawer.

Identify the wall before you drill. Concrete: masonry bits, suitable anchors, check for embedded services, respect landlord, HOA, or building drilling rules. Brick: do not explode a face brick for a heavy counter; use a method rated for that masonry and that load. Stud framing: fasten into suitable structural members you have located, not into the plaster skin. Drywall or hollow plaster only: hollow-wall anchors have low, orientation-dependent capacity — they are the wrong primary support for a loaded counter. Tile or wet finish: drilling can crack tile and admit water; stop if you cannot keep the hole sealed. Unknown substrate: stop and identify it.

Scribing means tracing the wall contour onto the panel and cutting to match. Shims are thin wedges that fill a gap so a cleat sits level. Service access means you can still reach hoses, vents, valves, and plugs after the millwork is in.

French cleats still need a substrate that can take the load. Two screws into hollow plaster are not a French-cleat system. If you cannot name the wall, you do not have a fastening plan.

Where this goes wrong

Hearing “load-bearing” and driving long screws without knowing whether the finish is 3/4 in plaster on brick or a hollow partition.

Output

  • Substrate named or drilling halted
  • Fastener type matched to that substrate
  • Services in the wall considered

A dry fit is cheaper than a second sheet.

Dry-fit the counter blank. Sand edges so they cannot cut skin or hoses. Pre-drill cleats. If walls bow, scribe, then reinstall. Screws into millwork should not replace the maker’s machine feet or shipping restraints.

Do not trap inlet hoses, drain hoses, or cords behind a fixed top you cannot lift without unscrewing a kitchen’s worth of hardware. A removable top or an access hatch next to the shutoffs is part of leak protection, not a luxury.

Organisation happens after the structure is stable and the machines can still come out. Bins do not fix a rocking cleat.

Where this goes wrong

Gluing a top down over the only shutoff access in the apartment.

Output

  • Dry fit done on the governing width
  • Hoses and shutoffs reachable after the top is on
  • No sharp unsanded edges in the splash zone

Chapter

Stacked And Side-By-Side Setups

By the end of this chapter, you will have one verified appliance layout. Keep front-load, top-load, stacked, side-by-side, and washer-only as separate problems. Output: a layout choice locked to manuals and tenure.

In this apartment

Ravi’s alcove is 34 inch wide with a bowed wall. He has a maker-approved stack kit for his exact washer and dryer models. Lease and HOA: no new exterior vent.

Stack only with an approved kit, and never on homemade millwork.

Typical combined stack height often lands near 1880–78 inch, but you must use the kit and both manuals. Confirm overhead clearance, service access to both units, and the removal path. A gas dryer, where one exists, adds fuel and combustion requirements — that is not a DIY vent-and-shelf problem.

In a rental, prefer no-drill or limited-fastener millwork beside the stack, not a platform the machines stand on unless that platform is designed for the load. Vibration pads belong only if the appliance maker allows them on that floor. Soft foam pads are the wrong answer for most washers.

Shelves beside a stack must not block lint access, controls, or the path to pull the pair. If the dryer is vented, the duct stays short, smooth, and cleanable — millwork does not pinch it.

Do this

  1. Confirm the stacking kit part number against both model numbers.
  2. Measure combined height and top clearance from the manuals.
  3. Sketch shelves that come off without moving the stack, or that leave a pull-out path.

Where this goes wrong

Resting a dryer on a plywood shelf because the alcove was too narrow for side-by-side.

Output

  • Approved kit confirmed
  • Gas dryer, if any, handed to a qualified person
  • Lint and service access open

In this apartment

Anjali owns a 62 inch alcove in Seattle. Front-load washer and vented dryer, side by side. An electrician already confirmed the existing sockets.

Width, two door swings, and a folding top that does not carry the machines.

Full-size cabinets are often near 27–28 in wide each, but your two stickers win. Leave the side and rear clearances the manuals name. A folding top sits above the higher of the two tops plus the maker’s top clearance — not 1 inch because a blog said so.

A center divider under a folding top can make hamper lanes only if it does not block hoses or prevent pulling a machine. Fasten into identified structure. This is the permanent level: owner approval, and a carpenter or electrician if the wall or circuit is unclear.

Do not copy the 62 inch width onto someone else’s cut list. It is Anjali’s opening.

Do this

  1. Write both cabinet widths plus both door swings.
  2. Set counter height from the manuals, not from a generic 38-inch washer.
  3. Draw hamper lanes that still allow a 32 in straight pull-out if that is your service path.

Where this goes wrong

A 27 inch-deep top that crushes the vent elbow behind the dryer.

Output

  • Both manuals’ side and rear clearances on the sketch
  • Counter not used as an appliance stand
  • Owner-level fastening matched to substrate

Chapter

Common Mistakes In The Laundry Alcove

By the end of this chapter, you will have a short list of stops that save the build. Each mistake is laundry-specific. Output: the stops written on the measurement sheet.

Sealed edges and a drip pan do not replace plumbing that can be shut off.

Prime every cut. Use materials from the moisture table. A drip pan is appropriate only where the appliance and floor allow it, and where you can still level the machine and reach a drain if the pan has one. Caulk is for splash. It is not a hidden-leak strategy behind a fixed panel.

Inlet hoses: inspect and replace on the interval the hose or machine maker names. Shutoff valves must stay reachable. Drain hoses must sit at the height the manual names, without kinks, unapproved extensions, or hanging unsecured.

After any move or reconnection, run a controlled short fill and watch joints before you leave the room. If you cannot see the connections because millwork hides them, the millwork is wrong.

Where this goes wrong

Calling the job waterproof because the counter got two coats of enamel.

Output

  • Hose condition and shutoffs on the inspection list
  • Drip pan decided yes/no from the appliance and floor, not from habit
  • Leak check planned after reconnection

Hollow-wall anchors are not a counter foundation.

Heavy folding tops and loaded shelves need structure you have identified. Drywall-only or loose plaster is a stop, not a challenge to buy bigger plastic plugs.

If the wall is concrete or brick, the drill, the anchor, and the load all change. If you do not own the wall, limited-fastener work still needs permission and a patch plan.

Missing a stud in a framed wall is a reason to move the cleat or add a spreader that hits two members — not a reason to hang the same load from gypsum.

Where this goes wrong

A row of hollow-wall anchors behind a 3/4 in top full of wet laundry.

Output

  • Substrate identified
  • Permission recorded if you do not own the wall
  • Primary load on structure, not on finish skin

The weekend can end at “sealed parts,” not at “in service.”

Thin coats. Labelled dry times. Do not load the top until the finish can take water and abrasion as the can describes.

If Sunday night arrives and the enamel is still soft, use the machines as they sat before, with millwork staged beside the alcove. That is still a completed planning-and-prep weekend.

High-build coats stay tacky in monsoon humidity. Plan for it instead of blasting a fan into wet solvent you have not read the label for.

Where this goes wrong

Declaring the alcove done while paint transfers onto dark clothes.

Output

  • Coat count and dry times on the schedule
  • MVP stop defined if cure overruns
  • No wet laundry on an uncured top

If you cannot pull the machine or open the lid, the millwork failed.

Leave reach to filters, lint paths, valves, the plug, and the drain. Removable panels beat fixed boxes in tight US laundry closets.

Review the installation guidelines for access points before you cut uprights. Future you will service this without demolishing the weekend’s work.

A stacked pair needs a path that does not require unscrewing a built-in wardrobe’s worth of sides.

Where this goes wrong

A face frame that looks like a kitchen, covering the only lint door.

Output

  • Lint, valves, plug, and drain reachable
  • Removal path drawn
  • Fixed vs removable panels decided

Do not move a washer or dryer alone with a towel and hope.

Read the appliance’s moving and transport instructions. Disconnect utilities using the proper procedure — water off, hoses drained, plug out, gas only by a qualified person. Secure drums and components as the maker requires (shipping bolts or transit bars on many washers). Use appropriate equipment and enough people for the mass of the machine. Protect flooring. Do not pinch hoses or cords. Reinstall, level, and reconnect per the manuals. Check for leaks and abnormal operation before normal use.

Cardboard is not a substitute for appliances sliders or a proper trolley when the machine is heavy or the floor is fragile. If you cannot recruit help or hire movers, postpone the pull-out and work around the machines.

Relevelling is not a coin trick. Follow the maker’s feet procedure. Then do a short controlled cycle.

Do this

Conversion: 25 mm is about 1 inch. Use inches on the cut list. Do not mix millimetres into an inch sheet. Photograph the labeled alcove.

Incomplete sentences belong in the bin. If a template line has no number, it is not measured.

Where this goes wrong

Using the 60 inch mid width for the top because it was the largest number on the page.

Output

  • Worked example copied, then overwritten with your inches
  • Governing values circled and mapped to parts
  • Photo of the labeled alcove stored

By the end of this chapter, you will have a three-stop US home-center map: lumber and sheet goods, fasteners, then paint and sealant. Home Depot, Lowe’s, and Menards are examples. Aisle numbers and SKUs change — still walk those three zones in order, and still match anchors to the wall you identified.

In this apartment

Ravi shops a Home Depot near Austin for a compact apartment laundry closet.

Start at lumber and the panel saw, then hardware, then paint.

At Home Depot, start at lumber and sheet goods — usually the back wall near the panel saw. Find 3/4 in sanded plywood for a folding top and 1/2 in plywood or MDF for shelves. Sight each sheet for bow and crushed corners. Ask kerf, minimum rip, and how many cuts they will make.

Finish at adhesives and caulk: construction adhesive if your method uses it, plus mildew-resistant flexible caulk. Do not treat this store’s aisle numbers as universal.

Do this

  2. Confirm panel-saw kerf and minimum rip before they cut.
  3. Record SKUs for fasteners and caulk.

Weekend Checklist And Final Inspection

By the end of this chapter, you will have a usable weekend checklist and a final inspection you must pass before treating the alcove as finished. Output: a signed inspection, or a list of stops.

In this apartment

The checklist keeps millwork inside the envelope and inside the manuals.

Use this list to reduce decision fatigue, not to force a 48-hour miracle. Core success is machines that still open, drain, and sit still, plus millwork that does not fight them.

Budget line: millwork ≤ $200 unless you consciously raise it. Still excludes trades and appliances.

If you fall behind, keep path lighting for the wet floor, reachable shutoffs, and a working washer. Drop the fourth shelf.

Finishing the paint and skipping the leak check because guests were coming.

A checkpoint is permission to continue — or to stop.

Ticking boxes you did not perform because the section title looked familiar.

Do not use the finished alcove until the inspection passes.

Final inspection, before normal use: appliance clearances and full door or lid operation; counter and shelves stable under a realistic lean and a full basket (not a person standing on them); fasteners and supports tight on the structure you named; water shutoffs and drain reachable; electrical plug accessible, no unsuitable cords; no blocked vents, lint paths, or service access; no sharp exposed edges; finishes and sealants cured as labeled; controlled leak check after reconnection; a clear removal or service path for the machines.
